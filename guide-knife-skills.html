Knife Skills: The Cuts That Cover Everything

Cooking guide · 7 min read

Nothing speeds up cooking — or makes it safer and more enjoyable — like being comfortable with a knife. You don't need to chop like a TV chef. You need a sharp knife, a steady grip, and five basic cuts. Get those, and prep stops being the slow, slightly nervous part of dinner.

Before any cut: three fundamentals

  • A sharp knife is a safe knife. Dull blades slip and require force; sharp ones go where you aim. A honing steel before each session and a real sharpening a few times a year make a huge difference.
  • Steady the board. Put a damp paper towel or thin cloth under your cutting board so it can't slide. This one trick prevents most kitchen accidents.
  • Grip the blade, not just the handle. Pinch the base of the blade between thumb and forefinger and wrap the rest around the handle. It feels odd for a day, then gives you far more control.

The claw: how to keep your fingertips

Curl the fingertips of your guiding hand under, so your knuckles face the blade and the flat of the knife rests lightly against them. The knife literally can't reach your fingertips. Move your knuckles back slowly as you cut. Go slow until the motion is automatic — speed is a byproduct of good form, never the goal.

The five cuts that cover almost everything

1. The rough chop

Irregular, bite-sized pieces. Perfect for vegetables headed into a stock, a long braise, or anything you'll blend — precision is wasted there. The fastest cut, and often the right one.

2. The dice (small, medium, large)

Even cubes. The trick is to first cut the item into flat-sided planks, then into sticks, then crosswise into cubes. Even sizing matters because it means even cooking — a small dice of onion melts into a sauce, while a large dice holds up in a chunky stew.

3. The slice

Thin, even pieces — for stir-fries, sautés, and anything that cooks fast. For meat, slice against the grain (across the visible muscle fibers) to shorten them, which is the secret to tender bites.

4. The mince

Very fine pieces, for garlic, ginger, chiles, and herbs you want distributed evenly. Slice first, then rock the knife over the pile, pivoting on the tip, until fine. A pinch of salt helps mince garlic into almost a paste.

5. The chiffonade

Thin ribbons of leafy herbs or greens like basil and mint. Stack the leaves, roll them into a tight cigar, and slice crosswise. It looks elegant and keeps delicate herbs from bruising.

Prep smarter, not just faster

  • Cut a thin slice off a round vegetable to create a flat, stable base before you keep going.
  • Group prep by cook time: things that go in together can be cut together.
  • Keep a "garbage bowl" on the counter so scraps don't crowd your board.

One knife to start

You can do 90% of home cooking with a single 8-inch chef's knife. Add a small paring knife for detail work and a serrated knife for bread and tomatoes, and you're set for years. A pricey block of knives you never learn to use is far less valuable than one good blade you keep sharp.
